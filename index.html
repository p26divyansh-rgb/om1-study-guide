1 Introduction to Operations Management

Operations = the processes that produce goods and services. A process is a fundamental activity that uses resources to transform inputs into outputs.

Transformation model INPUT (Material, Machines, Labor, Management, Capital) → TRANSFORMATION PROCESS → OUTPUT (Goods, Services) → Feedback loop back to input

Six types of transformation: Physical (manufacturing), Locational (transportation/warehousing), Exchange (retail), Physiological (healthcare), Psychological (entertainment), Informational (communications).

OM's Four Premises

  1. All work is a process.
  2. All processes can be improved.
  3. Processes are improved by making them better, faster, cheaper, and stronger.
  4. Improved processes add more value to customers, shareholders, employees, and society.

The Three Core Business Functions

Marketing
Generates demand.
Operations
Creates the product/service.
Finance/Accounting
Obtains funds & tracks money.

Why Study OM?

  • It's one of the 3 major functions of every organization.
  • OM is typically the costliest part of an organization (biggest lever on profit).
  • Efficiency frontier concept: a firm wants to (a) eliminate inefficiencies to reach the industry frontier, (b) make smart trade-offs along the frontier (e.g., wait time vs. labor utilization), and (c) redesign operations to push the frontier outward on multiple dimensions at once.

2 Basic OM Terminology & Little's Law High-yield

This is the single most tested block of the course — almost every numerical problem (queueing, capacity, line balancing) is built on these definitions.

Core Process Flow Measures

Flow Rate / Throughput (R)Average number of flow units passing through the process per unit timeunits/time
Flow Time / Throughput Time / Lead Time (T)Average time a unit spends within the system (from entry to exit)time
Inventory / WIP (I)Average number of flow units present within the process boundaries at any timeunits

UtilizationFlow Rate / Capacity — how much of available capacity is actually used%

Little's Law — THE most important formula in the course

Little's Law I = R × T (Inventory = Throughput/Flow Rate × Flow Time) Rearranged: T = I / R R = I / T Turnover (inventory turns) = R / I = 1 / T
Worked Example — Material Flow

A restaurant processes 5,000 kg of potatoes/week. Average raw-potato inventory = 2,500 kg. Find average flow time.

R = 5,000 kg/wk, I = 2,500 kg → T = I/R = 2,500/5,000 = 0.5 week

Worked Example — Customer Flow

Restaurant serves 1,500 customers/day (15-hr day → 100/hr). On average 75 customers are inside. Find average time spent and turnover.

R = 100/hr, I = 75 → T = 75/100 = 0.75 hr = 45 min. Turnover = R/I = 100/75 = 1.33/hr.

Worked Example — Job Flow (solve for I)

An insurance office processes 10,000 claims/year; average processing time = 3 weeks (50-week year). Find average claims "in process".

R = 10,000/yr, T = 3/50 yr → I = R×T = 10,000 × 3/50 = 600 claims

Consistency check (classic trick question)

A GM says inventory turns 3×/year, and everything clears the dock within 6 weeks. Consistent?

Turnover = 3/yr → T = 1/3 year ≈ 17.3 weeks. But claimed T = 6 weeks. 17.3 ≠ 6 → statements are inconsistent. Always convert to the same time unit before comparing!

Customer Order Decoupling Point (CODP)

The CODP is the point in the value chain up to which a customer's actual order penetrates (everything before is driven by forecast/planning; everything after is driven by actual demand). Moving the CODP upstream (toward MTS) improves delivery speed but increases inventory risk; moving it downstream (toward ETO) reduces inventory risk but increases lead time.

Market-driven requirements pushing DP downstream (later):
required delivery reliability, required delivery time, predictability & specificity of demand
Process/stock constraints pushing DP upstream (earlier):
long lead times, high changeover costs, poor process control, need for high reliability, cost of stock-holding, risk of obsolescence

5 Facility Layouts & Assembly Line Design/Balancing Numerical-heavy

Cellular LayoutDifferent machine types grouped into a "cell" that produces a family of similar productsFurniture manufacturing cell

Labor Specialization on the Assembly Line

Advantages
Management: rapid training, easy recruiting, high output from simple repetitive work, close control of workflow.
Labor: little education needed, easy to learn the job.
Disadvantages
Management: reduced improvement (limited worker perspective), low flexibility to change the line, difficulty controlling quality.
Labor: boredom, little control over pace → fatigue/frustration, little chance to advance.

Spatial Configurations

Straight Line Design vs. U-Shaped Design. U-shaped lines let one worker handle stations at both the start and end of the line, improve visibility/communication between workers, and make it easier to rebalance the line when demand (and hence required staffing) changes.

Assembly Line Balancing — the 8-Step Procedure Must know

  1. Draw the precedence diagram (tasks + predecessor relationships).
  2. Determine required workstation cycle time = Production time available per day / Required output per day.
  3. Determine theoretical minimum number of workstations = (Sum of all task times) / Cycle time.
  4. Select an assignment rule (e.g., longest task time first, most followers first).
  5. Assign tasks one at a time to the first workstation until the sum of task times equals the cycle time, or no other task is feasible (predecessor/time constraint).
  6. Repeat step 5 for the next workstation until all tasks are assigned.
  7. Compute line efficiency.
  8. If efficiency is unsatisfactory, rebalance using a different rule.
Line Balancing Formulas Cycle Time (CT) = Production time available per day / Required units per day Theoretical Min. Workstations (N) = ΣTask times / Cycle Time (round UP) Line Efficiency = ΣTask times / (Actual no. of workstations × Cycle Time) Balance Delay = 1 − Efficiency Labour Content = Sum of all labour (task) times to make one unit Available Labour Time = Number of workers × Cycle Time Labour Utilization = Labour Content / Available Labour Time
Zoning & personnel constraints (as tested in the 2025 "Artisan Shirt" question): a single workstation often cannot mix tasks from different process zones (e.g., Cutting/Stitching/Finishing), and a workstation's skill requirement = the highest-skilled task assigned to it (a senior worker CAN do junior tasks, not vice-versa). Always re-verify max daily output, labor cost/unit, and any KPI after re-balancing.

Process Batch vs. Transfer Batch

  • Process Batch: number of units processed together at one stage before changing over to a different product/batch.
  • Transfer Batch: number of units physically moved together from one stage to the next (can be smaller than the process batch — moving in smaller transfer batches overlaps stages and reduces flow time, even though process batch/capacity stays the same).

6 Mass Customization MCQ favorite

Mass Production aims for efficiency through stability/control — standardized, low-cost, long product life cycles for large homogeneous markets. Mass Customization aims for variety & customization through flexibility and quick response — customized, high-quality, short product life cycles for fragmented/heterogeneous markets. Customization = product variety, so customers must be involved somewhere in the process.

7 Toyota Production System, Lean & JIT Very high-yield

TPS rests on two pillars: Just-in-Time (JIT) and Jidoka.

The 3 M's of Waste

Muda (無駄) — Waste: anything not adding value. 7 classic types: overproduction, waiting, transportation, inefficient/over-processing, excess inventory, unnecessary motion, defects.
Muri — Overburden: pushing people/equipment beyond natural limits.
Mura — Unevenness: inconsistent workload/demand fluctuations. (Heijunka directly fights Mura.)

Push vs. Pull Systems

  • Push: material is pushed into downstream stations regardless of whether resources/demand are ready (produce-to-forecast).
  • Pull: material is pulled into a station only when actually needed — driven by real, downstream demand. JIT = producing only what is needed, when needed, in the amount needed. Kanban cards are the visual signal that triggers a pull.

Heijunka — Production Leveling

Instead of large batches of one model at a time (AAA→BB→CC), Toyota interleaves a mixed sequence each day (A→B→A→C→A→B) that matches the actual mix of customer demand. This levels workload for the plant and its suppliers, and combats Mura and Muri.

Jidoka — "Automation with a Human Touch"

Machines/workers can detect an abnormality and stop the line immediately so defects are caught at the source, rather than passed downstream. Origin: Sakichi Toyoda's automatic loom that stopped itself when a thread broke.

  • Andon — a visual signal (lamp/cord) a worker pulls to flag a problem and stop the line: Red = stoppage, Yellow = call for help, Green = normal.
  • Poka-Yoke — mistake-proofing devices (sensors, jigs, alignment pins) designed to prevent defects before they occur (goal: zero defects) — different from Statistical Quality Control, which finds defects after they occur.

Setup Time Reduction (SMED thinking)

  • Internal setup: must be done while the machine is stopped.
  • External setup: can be done while the machine is still running.
  • Strategy: (1) convert internal setup to external where possible, (2) then streamline what remains internal. Lower setups enable smaller batch sizes without losing throughput.

Kaizen, 5 Whys, PDCA

  • Kaizen: continuous, incremental improvement; a "Kaizen event" is a focused ~5-day team effort to fix a specific process.
  • 5 Whys: ask "why" repeatedly (typically 5×) to drill down from a symptom to its root cause, rather than treating surface symptoms.
  • PDCA / Deming Cycle: Plan (go to Gemba — the real place — observe the real thing/Gembutsu, get real facts/Genjitsu) → Do (implement, create models of excellence) → Check (align with long-term direction) → Act (sustain & accelerate improvements).

The 14 Toyota Way Principles (grouped)

  1. Long-term philosophy over short-term financial gains.
  2. Continuous process flow to surface problems (eliminate Muda).
  3. Pull systems / JIT — avoid overproduction.
  4. Heijunka — level the workload (fight Muda, Muri, Mura).
  5. Jidoka — stop and fix problems to get quality right the first time.
  6. Standardized work as the basis for continuous improvement (+ Poka-Yoke).
  7. Visual controls / 5S so no problem is hidden.
  8. Reliable, thoroughly-tested technology that serves people and process — not tech for its own sake.
  9. Grow leaders who live the philosophy and teach it.
  10. Develop exceptional people & teams who both do the work and improve it.
  11. Respect & develop suppliers as long-term partners, not adversaries.
  12. Genchi Genbutsu — "go and see" for yourself rather than relying only on reports.
  13. Decide slowly by consensus, implement rapidly (nemawashi).
  14. Become a learning organization through relentless reflection (Hansei) and Kaizen.

Total Productive Maintenance (TPM) & Lean Attributes

Lean producers: use JIT to cut inventory, aim for a perfect part every time, reduce space needs, build close/educated supplier relationships, eliminate non-value-add activities, build workforce flexibility (fewer job classes, cross-training), and apply TPM (proactive/preventive equipment maintenance to avoid breakdowns that would stall a low-inventory, tightly-coupled line).

8 Supplier Relationships

Traditional/Adversarial Vendor Management
Multiple competing suppliers played off each other, negotiated purely on price, short-term contracts, low information sharing, suppliers "beaten up" on performance (e.g., pressuring a supplier to abandon a project).
Toyota's Lean / Keiretsu Model
Long-term partnerships, trust, mutual knowledge-sharing, suppliers treated as an extension of the firm. Toyota sets high standards and challenges suppliers, but also helps them improve — testing commitment with smaller orders before scaling up to a permanent relationship (from Toyota Way Principle 11).
Exam angle: identify whether a described buyer-supplier scenario reflects adversarial/traditional vendor management vs. a Toyota-style Keiretsu partnership, and state the trade-offs (short-term cost savings vs. long-term quality/innovation/resilience).

9 Strategic Capacity Management Lead/Lag is a favorite

Operations Strategy = the plan for reconciling market requirements with operations resources. Four decision areas: Capacity, Supply network, Process technology, Development & organisation — judged against 5 customer priorities: quality, speed, dependability, flexibility, cost.

Capacity = the throughput/maximum amount a facility can hold, receive, store, or produce in a period. It sets fixed costs and determines whether demand can be met.

Three Time Horizons for Capacity Decisions

HorizonWhat you can doExamples
Short-rangeUse existing capacity better (capacity itself is hard to change)Schedule jobs, schedule staff, allocate machinery
Intermediate-rangeModerately adjust capacitySubcontract, add shifts/personnel/equipment, build or use inventory
Long-rangeMake big, slow-to-reverse capacity changesBuild new facilities, buy long-lead-time equipment

Strategic Capacity Decisions

  • What type of fixed capacity? Manual labor vs. semi-automated vs. fully automated.
  • When/how much to expand? (see lead/lag below)
  • Where to locate capacity? Labour, raw materials, market proximity, transport, infrastructure, taxes, political risk, environment.

Managing Demand vs. Capacity Mismatches

SituationShort-term fixLong-term fix
Demand > CapacityRaise prices, extend lead times/waitIncrease capacity
Capacity > DemandStimulate demand via marketing/product changesDivest / repurpose capacity
Seasonal demandOffer complementary products that sell in the off-seasonBuild flexible/multi-use capacity

Economies & Diseconomies of Scale

As output grows toward an optimal size, average unit cost falls (fixed costs spread over more units) = economies of scale. Beyond the optimal size, average cost starts rising again (coordination/complexity costs) = diseconomies of scale. Classic example: a 25-room motel has higher unit cost than a 50-room motel, but a 75-room motel may again be less efficient to manage. There's a "sweet spot" size.

Key Capacity Planning Considerations

  1. Forecast demand accurately.
  2. Understand technology & capacity increments (capacity often comes in fixed-size "chunks" — you can't buy half a machine).
  3. Find the optimal operating volume.
  4. Build flexibility for future change.

Should Capacity LEAD or LAG Demand? Frequently tested

StrategyProsCons
Lead demand (build ahead of confirmed need)Captures fast-growing demand, avoids lost sales, grabs early market shareRisk of costly unused/idle capacity if demand disappoints
Lag demand (expand only after demand is confirmed)Lower risk, can adopt newer tech later, avoids overspendingMay fail to meet demand short-term, risk of losing market share
Middle groundLead in small incremental steps, or track an average level (sometimes ahead, sometimes behind)Balances risk vs. responsiveness
Match strategy to product type: Custom/low-volume/made-to-order operations (bespoke tailoring, custom engineering) should generally LEAD demand and stay flexible. Standard/high-volume/made-to-stock commodities (bottled water) should generally LAG demand and focus on low cost.

Capacity Needs Across the Product Life Cycle

StageWhat's happeningCapacity/Ops focus
IntroductionProduct is newGrab market share; frequent design changes; short, costly runs
GrowthSales rising fastIncrease capacity; improve forecasting; strengthen distribution
MaturitySales level offOptimize capacity; shift to cost-cutting, long stable runs
DeclineSales fallingCost control critical; industry often over-capacity; cut weak products

Examples: floppy disks = classic decline stage; flat-screen monitors/DVDs were once "introduction".

10 Bullwhip Effect & Supply Chain Coordination Guaranteed MCQ topic

Bullwhip Effect: a small change in actual end-customer demand causes increasingly amplified swings in orders as you move upstream through the supply chain (Customer → Retailer → Distributor → Manufacturer → Supplier). Classic demonstration: the Beer Game.

Numeric feel for it

Customer demand rises 100 → 110 (only +10%). Retailer, fearing more growth, orders 125. Distributor sees retailer's jump and orders 150. Manufacturer sees that and produces 180. A tiny real signal becomes a huge upstream swing.

Symptoms

  • Excessive inventory ("just in case" buffers)
  • Poor demand forecasts
  • Insufficient capacity when a surge hits
  • Long backlogs

Causes (know these — classic MCQ list)

  1. Demand forecast updating — each stage forecasts off the orders it receives, not real customer sales, compounding errors.
  2. Order batching — infrequent large orders (0-0-0-400) instead of steady small ones (100-100-100-100) make demand look spikier than it is.
  3. Price fluctuations / promotions — discounts cause forward-buying, misread as a genuine demand increase, followed by a post-promotion crash.
  4. Lack of information sharing — no visibility into true end-customer demand.
  5. Long lead times / too many intermediaries — long lead times push firms to over-order "just in case"; every extra intermediary adds distortion.
  6. Disorganization / lack of coordination — each firm reacts independently to demand changes.
  7. Free returns / order cancellation — firms over-order, planning to cancel/return the excess, creating phantom demand spikes.

Remedies

  1. Use actual point-of-sale data instead of each tier re-forecasting independently.
  2. Break large batches into smaller, more frequent orders.
  3. Stabilize prices (Every-Day-Low-Price over frequent promotions).
  4. Improve information sharing across the chain (sales, inventory, forecasts, production plans).
  5. Respond to demand changes proportionately, not dramatically.
The core Beer Game/exam lesson: mitigating the bullwhip effect is fundamentally about improving information sharing and collaboration across the whole chain so every tier can see real end-customer demand — not about ordering in isolation or stockpiling more inventory.

Pillars of Supply-Chain Coordination

Trust · Coordination · Partnership · Integration — the antidote to the bullwhip effect is treating the supply chain as one coordinated system (as in Toyota's Keiretsu model) rather than a sequence of isolated, self-interested links.

11 Project Management: CPM & Crashing Always a full numerical question

A project is a unique, one-time set of operations to accomplish objectives within a limited time/cost frame.

Key Terms

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)
Precedence relations
Network diagram
Early Start / Forward Pass
Late Start / Backward Pass
Gantt chart
Slack / Float
Critical Path
Critical Path Method — core formulas FORWARD PASS (left to right): computes Earliest Start (ES) and Earliest Finish (EF) ES(activity) = max(EF of all its predecessors) [ES = 0 for start activities] EF(activity) = ES + Duration BACKWARD PASS (right to left): computes Latest Start (LS) and Latest Finish (LF) LF(activity) = min(LS of all its successors) [LF = Project duration, for end activities] LS(activity) = LF − Duration SLACK (Float) = LS − ES = LF − EF CRITICAL PATH = the longest path through the network = path where slack = 0 for every activity PROJECT DURATION = length (sum of durations) of the critical path
Exam fact (was literally MCQ #10 in 2025): the total slack of any activity on the critical path is always zero — that's the definition of "critical." The critical path itself is the longest path (not shortest), and represents the minimum possible project duration.
Worked Example — Forward/Backward Pass

Activities: A(3, no pred) → D(5, pred A) → G(4, pred B,D,E); B(6, no pred); C(2, no pred) → E(2, pred C); A → F(7).

Forward pass gives EF: A=3, B=6, C=2, D=8, E=4, F=10, G=12 → project duration = 12 weeks.

Backward pass from LF=12 gives slack: A=0, D=0, G=0 (critical path A–D–G); B has slack 2; C has slack 4; E has slack 4; F has slack 2.

Critical Path = A → D → G, Duration = 12 weeks.

Project Crashing

Crashing = spending extra money to shorten a critical activity's duration, in order to shrink total project time (e.g., to avoid a late-delivery penalty).

Crashing rules 1. Only CRITICAL activities are worth crashing (shortening a non-critical activity doesn't shorten the project). 2. Crash period-by-period (e.g., one week at a time), re-checking the critical path after each step. 3. Pick the critical activity with the lowest Cost Slope = (Crash Cost − Normal Cost) / (Normal Time − Crash Time). 4. Watch out for PARALLEL critical paths — once multiple paths become simultaneously critical, you may need to crash one activity on EACH parallel path (or a shared activity) to gain even one more week.
Worked Example — Crashing

Critical path A–D–G (12 weeks). Cost slopes: A=$5,000/wk, D=$4,000/wk, G=$7,000/wk. Cheapest is D → crash D by 1 week → project time = 11 weeks, cost = Normal($61,000) + $4,000 = $65,000.

Crash D again → path becomes 10 weeks, but now 3 paths are simultaneously critical: A-D-G, B-G, A-F. To shrink further, cheapest combo is crashing F and G together ($3,000+$7,000) rather than crashing 3 separate activities — always check whether one shared activity (like A) can shorten multiple paths at once before crashing them individually.

Uncertainty & Risk in Projects

  • Every activity-time estimate rests on assumptions — CPM outputs are only as good as those assumptions.
  • The critical path is dynamic — it can shift as activities slip or are crashed.
  • A project manager's job goes well beyond running the CPM calculation.
Risk categoryExamples
FinancialPenalty clauses, exchange-rate swings
MarketingDemand/market changes, brand image
TechnologyTie-up/partner risk, obsolescence
Operations/ExecutionResource availability, delays in activity durations

Managing risk: maintain redundancy of resources, monitor & report frequently, transfer risk (e.g., contracts/insurance), and share risk with partners.

12 Worked Numericals: Inventory Build-Up & Batch Capacity

Inventory Build-Up Diagrams (Queueing over time)

These questions give a time-varying arrival rate and a (possibly changing) service/processing capacity, and ask you to track the queue over time. Core method:

Queue tracking logic For each time interval: Change in Queue = Arrival Rate − Service (Capacity) Rate [if positive → queue grows; if negative → queue shrinks, floor at 0] Queue(end of interval) = Queue(start) + (Arrival Rate − Capacity) × interval length Peak Queue = the highest point on this cumulative build-up curve Longest wait = found by matching a specific unit's arrival point against the cumulative "departures" curve (This is precisely a Little's Law style diagram: the vertical gap between the cumulative-arrivals and cumulative-departures curves = instantaneous inventory/queue; the horizontal gap = wait time for the unit arriving at that point.)
Style of question (2025 exam: Delhi T3 security)

Arrivals vary in three time bands (25/min, then 40/min, then 20/min); capacity = 5 lanes × 5/min = 25/min, with 2 extra lanes triggered once queue > 150, and a machine going offline for a period. You must (a) build the cumulative arrivals vs. departures diagram, (b) find peak queue & when it occurs, (c) find the longest wait and which arrival experiences it, and (d) evaluate a "proactive" policy change (open extra lanes earlier) by comparing peak queue and max wait under both policies.

Approach: break the timeline into pieces wherever arrival rate OR capacity changes; track cumulative queue at each breakpoint; the moment queue crosses a policy threshold (e.g., 150), capacity changes for subsequent intervals too.

Batch Processing & Setup-Time Capacity (e.g., Gene Cattie Enterprises case)

When a single resource is shared by multiple products with a changeover/setup time, capacity depends on batch size:

Capacity with setup times Time per batch = Setup time + (Batch size × Run time per unit) Effective Capacity (units/time) = Batch size / Time per batch ⇒ Larger batches amortize the fixed setup time over more units → HIGHER effective capacity ⇒ Smaller batches → more frequent setups → LOWER effective capacity, but LOWER flow time / WIP (Little's Law trade-off!)
This is the classic "batch size trade-off": bigger batches raise throughput capacity (fewer setups) but increase average inventory/WIP and flow time (Little's Law: I = R×T — units sit longer waiting for a full batch to complete). Line-balancing and CODP thinking both connect back to this same batch-size vs. flow-time trade-off.

Reading a full production system as a chain of resources

  1. Compute the (batch-adjusted) capacity of each resource/department independently.
  2. The system bottleneck = the resource with the lowest capacity once you account for the actual product mix moving through it (a resource used by multiple products may have less capacity "left over" for one product than it looks like in isolation).
  3. System throughput = bottleneck's capacity (you can never produce faster than your slowest necessary resource).
  4. Use Little's Law (I = R×T) with the actual inventory data at each stage to compute total average flow time through the whole factory.
