9 Strategic Capacity Management Lead/Lag favorite

Topic 9 of 12 · Time horizons, economies of scale, and the classic lead-vs-lag capacity strategy question.

Operations Strategy = the plan for reconciling market requirements with operations resources. Four decision areas: Capacity, Supply network, Process technology, Development & organisation — judged against 5 customer priorities: quality, speed, dependability, flexibility, cost.

Capacity = the throughput/maximum amount a facility can hold, receive, store, or produce in a period. It sets fixed costs and determines whether demand can be met.

Three Time Horizons for Capacity Decisions

HorizonWhat you can doExamples
Short-rangeUse existing capacity better (capacity itself is hard to change)Schedule jobs, schedule staff, allocate machinery
Intermediate-rangeModerately adjust capacitySubcontract, add shifts/personnel/equipment, build or use inventory
Long-rangeMake big, slow-to-reverse capacity changesBuild new facilities, buy long-lead-time equipment

Strategic Capacity Decisions

  • What type of fixed capacity? Manual labor vs. semi-automated vs. fully automated.
  • When/how much to expand? (see lead/lag below)
  • Where to locate capacity? Labour, raw materials, market proximity, transport, infrastructure, taxes, political risk, environment.

Managing Demand vs. Capacity Mismatches

SituationShort-term fixLong-term fix
Demand > CapacityRaise prices, extend lead times/waitIncrease capacity
Capacity > DemandStimulate demand via marketing/product changesDivest / repurpose capacity
Seasonal demandOffer complementary products that sell in the off-seasonBuild flexible/multi-use capacity

Economies & Diseconomies of Scale

As output grows toward an optimal size, average unit cost falls (fixed costs spread over more units) = economies of scale. Beyond the optimal size, average cost starts rising again (coordination/complexity costs) = diseconomies of scale. Classic example: a 25-room motel has higher unit cost than a 50-room motel, but a 75-room motel may again be less efficient to manage. There's a "sweet spot" size.

Key Capacity Planning Considerations

  1. Forecast demand accurately.
  2. Understand technology & capacity increments (capacity often comes in fixed-size "chunks" — you can't buy half a machine).
  3. Find the optimal operating volume.
  4. Build flexibility for future change.

Should Capacity LEAD or LAG Demand? Frequently tested

StrategyProsCons
Lead demand (build ahead of confirmed need)Captures fast-growing demand, avoids lost sales, grabs early market shareRisk of costly unused/idle capacity if demand disappoints
Lag demand (expand only after demand is confirmed)Lower risk, can adopt newer tech later, avoids overspendingMay fail to meet demand short-term, risk of losing market share
Middle groundLead in small incremental steps, or track an average level (sometimes ahead, sometimes behind)Balances risk vs. responsiveness
Match strategy to product type: Custom/low-volume/made-to-order operations (bespoke tailoring, custom engineering) should generally LEAD demand and stay flexible. Standard/high-volume/made-to-stock commodities (bottled water) should generally LAG demand and focus on low cost.

Capacity Needs Across the Product Life Cycle

StageWhat's happeningCapacity/Ops focus
IntroductionProduct is newGrab market share; frequent design changes; short, costly runs
GrowthSales rising fastIncrease capacity; improve forecasting; strengthen distribution
MaturitySales level offOptimize capacity; shift to cost-cutting, long stable runs
DeclineSales fallingCost control critical; industry often over-capacity; cut weak products

Examples: floppy disks = classic decline stage; flat-screen monitors/DVDs were once "introduction".
