7 Toyota Production System, Lean & JIT Very high-yield

Topic 7 of 12 · Muda/Muri/Mura, Jidoka, Heijunka, 5S, Kaizen, 5 Whys, and all 14 Toyota Way principles.

TPS rests on two pillars: Just-in-Time (JIT) and Jidoka.

The 3 M's of Waste

Muda (無駄) — Waste: anything not adding value. 7 classic types: overproduction, waiting, transportation, inefficient/over-processing, excess inventory, unnecessary motion, defects.
Muri — Overburden: pushing people/equipment beyond natural limits.
Mura — Unevenness: inconsistent workload/demand fluctuations. (Heijunka directly fights Mura.)

Push vs. Pull Systems

  • Push: material is pushed into downstream stations regardless of whether resources/demand are ready (produce-to-forecast).
  • Pull: material is pulled into a station only when actually needed — driven by real, downstream demand. JIT = producing only what is needed, when needed, in the amount needed. Kanban cards are the visual signal that triggers a pull.

Heijunka — Production Leveling

Instead of large batches of one model at a time (AAA→BB→CC), Toyota interleaves a mixed sequence each day (A→B→A→C→A→B) that matches the actual mix of customer demand. This levels workload for the plant and its suppliers, and combats Mura and Muri.

Jidoka — "Automation with a Human Touch"

Machines/workers can detect an abnormality and stop the line immediately so defects are caught at the source, rather than passed downstream. Origin: Sakichi Toyoda's automatic loom that stopped itself when a thread broke.

  • Andon — a visual signal (lamp/cord) a worker pulls to flag a problem and stop the line: Red = stoppage, Yellow = call for help, Green = normal.
  • Poka-Yoke — mistake-proofing devices (sensors, jigs, alignment pins) designed to prevent defects before they occur (goal: zero defects) — different from Statistical Quality Control, which finds defects after they occur.

Setup Time Reduction (SMED thinking)

  • Internal setup: must be done while the machine is stopped.
  • External setup: can be done while the machine is still running.
  • Strategy: (1) convert internal setup to external where possible, (2) then streamline what remains internal. Lower setups enable smaller batch sizes without losing throughput.

Kaizen, 5 Whys, PDCA

  • Kaizen: continuous, incremental improvement; a "Kaizen event" is a focused ~5-day team effort to fix a specific process.
  • 5 Whys: ask "why" repeatedly (typically 5×) to drill down from a symptom to its root cause, rather than treating surface symptoms.
  • PDCA / Deming Cycle: Plan (go to Gemba — the real place — observe the real thing/Gembutsu, get real facts/Genjitsu) → Do (implement, create models of excellence) → Check (align with long-term direction) → Act (sustain & accelerate improvements).

The 14 Toyota Way Principles (grouped)

  1. Long-term philosophy over short-term financial gains.
  2. Continuous process flow to surface problems (eliminate Muda).
  3. Pull systems / JIT — avoid overproduction.
  4. Heijunka — level the workload (fight Muda, Muri, Mura).
  5. Jidoka — stop and fix problems to get quality right the first time.
  6. Standardized work as the basis for continuous improvement (+ Poka-Yoke).
  7. Visual controls / 5S so no problem is hidden.
  8. Reliable, thoroughly-tested technology that serves people and process — not tech for its own sake.
  9. Grow leaders who live the philosophy and teach it.
  10. Develop exceptional people & teams who both do the work and improve it.
  11. Respect & develop suppliers as long-term partners, not adversaries.
  12. Genchi Genbutsu — "go and see" for yourself rather than relying only on reports.
  13. Decide slowly by consensus, implement rapidly (nemawashi).
  14. Become a learning organization through relentless reflection (Hansei) and Kaizen.

Total Productive Maintenance (TPM) & Lean Attributes

Lean producers: use JIT to cut inventory, aim for a perfect part every time, reduce space needs, build close/educated supplier relationships, eliminate non-value-add activities, build workforce flexibility (fewer job classes, cross-training), and apply TPM (proactive/preventive equipment maintenance to avoid breakdowns that would stall a low-inventory, tightly-coupled line).
